## The number nobody checks

Search for "pet affiliate program" and you find dozens of articles repeating the same line: *"commissions up to 40%"*. None of them takes you to the document that actually defines the rate.

I went to the document. The [Amazon Associates Standard Commission Rules](https://associados.amazon.com.br/welcome/compensation) are public and list, category by category, what the associate earns per qualified purchase. Read on 16/11/2026:

- **Pet Shop: 11%**
- Food and Beverage, Health and Personal Care, Beauty: **13%**
- Books and eBooks: **10%**
- Amazon Devices (Echo, Fire TV, Kindle): **9.5%**
- Home, Furniture, Kitchen, Tools, Appliances: **8%**

Besides percentages, there are fixed-value reward events in categories such as digital subscriptions and credit cards — exact amounts live in the official terms and change with promotions.

None of this is an estimate. It is a table, with a reading date.

## What that changes in the math

Pet Shop at 11% means commission scales with the sale value: higher-ticket items return more, and the volume needed for any target depends on the category average. Numeric examples stay in the official terms.

That is the calculation nobody does before writing the guide. Forty percent sounds like ten times more, and the gap between 11% and 40% is not an optimization, it is a broken expectation.

## The bottleneck was not the rate

Our own funnel, measured on 16/11/2026 over the last 30 days:

- human visits in the tens across store and blog (the rest of the traffic is bots)
- no paid sales in the period
- Google presence still irrelevant: few impressions, fewer clicks

The problem is not the commission, the link or the offer. It is at the top: not enough people arrive. A new guide on a site with dozens of human visits a month is text almost nobody reads. And 11% of zero sales is still zero.

That reorders the priorities of any affiliate project: first **who reads**, then **what sells**, and only then **how much it pays**.

## The technical detail that nearly slipped

Pages carrying affiliate links did not point to a product: they pointed to a **search page** (`/s?k=term`). Attribution still works, but search is a weak-intent page: the reader lands on a list with varying prices and picks something else.

A product link has a single price, reviews and a buy button on the same screen. For a comparison guide, that is the difference between "check the prices" and "buy this one".

## What stays from this experiment

1. **Rates have a primary source.** Before publishing any commission number, read the program's rules, and date the reading.
2. **Commission is calculated in money, not percentage.** Translating the percentage into a concrete value prevents empty promises — but always use ranges and the official source, never fixed numbers.
3. **Traffic comes before monetization.** A site with dozens of visits a month does not have a conversion problem; it has a distribution problem.
4. **A search link does not replace a product link** on a purchase-intent page.

None of these four conclusions required a paid tool. They required reading the source, measuring our own funnel and accepting the number that showed up.
