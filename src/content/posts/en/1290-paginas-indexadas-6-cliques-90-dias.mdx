## The number I used to read as a grade

Search Console shows one number per domain: **indexed**. For a long time I read that number as a grade. This morning it said 1,290 and it looked good.

Then I asked the other question: how many clicks did those 1,290 pages produce in the last 90 days?

**Six.**

Not six thousand. Six.

Below is what I did next and what I would do differently if you are reading this before repeating the path.

## A single number is not a diagnosis

"Indexed / not indexed" is the summary of two long lists. Every row in the second list carries a `coverageState`, which is the reason Google gave. If you read only the total, you are reading a report card, not a report.

So I grouped the states instead of summing them. Same data, one line per reason:

```
indexed ................................. 1290
not indexed ............................. 1230
  alternate page with proper canonical tag 1025
  duplicate, Google chose different canonical 82
  404 ..................................... 49
  redirect ................................ 30
  crawled, currently not indexed ........... 18
  discovered, currently not indexed ........ 13
  noindex .................................. 7
  blocked by robots.txt .................... 6
  soft 404 ................................. 2
  blocked, other 4xx ....................... 1
```

2,520 known URLs. My five sitemaps declare 713 (593 blog, 84 store, 22 product site, 8 portfolio, 6 root). Which means: Google knows more URLs than I publish; the rest comes from links and cross-posts. That gap alone was worth knowing about.

## Bucket by bucket, what each one actually means

**1,025 "alternate page with proper canonical tag".** The biggest bucket and the least alarming. It means: this URL duplicates another, the canonical tag points to the right place, and Google agrees. It is the expected footprint of language versions, trailing-slash variants and cross-posts.

To be precise: I did **not** measure the exact composition of those 1,025. I know what the bucket means, I do not know what is inside it. Claiming a cause would be inventing one, and inventing a cause for a big number is how you spend a week fixing the wrong thing.

**82 "Google chose a different canonical".** The only bucket that is unambiguously a problem, and small enough to fix by hand. My page said "the canonical is A" and Google indexed B. Practical consequence: the URL you think ranks is not the one ranking. Your analytics, your internal links and your canonical tag all point at a page Google treats as a copy.

Fixing order: open the 82 and ask, for each one, *why would Google prefer B?* Usually B is the version with real external links, or the one that answers 200 without redirect hops, or the one without boilerplate content.

**95 URLs that should not be in a sitemap** (49 × 404, 30 × redirect, 7 × noindex, 6 × robots.txt, 2 × soft 404, 1 × other 4xx). Each one is a URL I asked Google to crawl and then refused to serve. A sitemap is a promise; 95 broken promises is crawl budget spent on nothing.

**31 "crawled or discovered, not indexed"** (18 + 13). Google saw the page and decided it was not worth indexing. That is a content signal, not a technical one. Resubmitting the sitemap does not fix it.

## The uncomfortable conclusion

1,290 pages indexed and 6 clicks means indexing was never my bottleneck. Indexing is a *gate*, not a *channel*. Getting through the gate with 1,290 pages and harvesting 6 clicks in 90 days means the bottleneck lives somewhere else: nobody searches for what I wrote, or the title does not match the search, or the search has no volume.

I had been treating "indexed" as a KPI because it was the number the panel put in the biggest font.

## What I actually changed

1. **Sitemap hygiene became a check, not a cleanup task.** Any URL in the sitemap that answers anything other than 200 leaves before the next deploy.
2. **I audit the 82 divergent ones by hand, every month.** It is small enough. A swapped canonical is cheap to find and expensive to ignore.
3. **I stopped reporting "indexed" on its own.** The report carries `indexed`, `clicks` and the main non-indexing reason. Three numbers, because one number hides the work.
4. **I kept the raw rows.** A bucket is a view over data I still have, so next month I can ask another question without re-collecting anything.

The code that made this possible is embarrassingly small. The work was deciding to look at the distribution instead of the total:

```
from collections import Counter

states = [row["coverageState"] for row in inspection_rows]
total = len(states)
for state, n in Counter(states).most_common():
    print(f"{n:5d}  {n/total:5.1%}  {state}")
```

That's it. No library, no dashboard, no vendor. If your panel shows a single number, group the rows yourself.

## If you take only one thing

A metric that only goes up is not measurement, it is mood. "Indexed pages" only goes up. "Clicks coming from indexed pages" can fall, and that is why it informs.

Next time a panel hands you a green number, ask what the distribution behind it looks like. The distribution is the report. The total is the summary someone wrote so you would stop asking.
