## The question that looked simple

Semantic retrieval has a defect baked into it: what you index is a single
conversation turn. A three-word question, a short answer, done. When a user
remembers a topic by context — *"that problem we had on Tuesday"* — the turn on
its own carries none of that.

The hypothesis was obvious: take the turn and its neighbours, concatenate them,
index the whole block. If the embedding starts seeing the context, search by
topic should hit more often. I built a configurable window of N turns on each
side and ran the LoCoMo benchmark, the collection I use to measure recall before
anything touches production.

What I did not expect was that the same change, measured three times, would give
three different verdicts — and that only the last one was right.

## Three measurements, three answers

The first pilot used a small subset of real items. The result was a sharp
regression: `hit@1` fell from 0.7895 to 0.0877 and `recall@10` from 0.8246 to
0.1228. The delta came out negative and the honest reading was: the window
destroys retrieval. I recorded the verdict as REJECTED and moved on.

But a delta that large smells like environment, not statistics. When a perfect
hit collapses to near zero, the symptom is a wiped index collection, an embedder
answering in the wrong shape, a silent timeout — not a text change that should
be neutral. I re-ran the measurement and the regression was an infrastructure
artifact: with a healthy path, the gain appeared and reproduced across three
consecutive runs. Verdict corrected to APPROVED on the pilot.

The third measurement ran on the full corpus, with cross-talk — different
conversations living in the same index. There the window genuinely failed:
`recall@10` went from 0.4669 to 0.3349, `mrr` from 0.3254 to 0.2046, and hitting
the correct conversation in first position dropped from 93.8% to 49.1%. Not an
artifact. The real effect of the change, visible only once the index had actual
competition.

## The root cause

The window concatenates neighbouring turns into one string and embeds the
result. The problem is that an embedding averages the signal of the whole block:
the more text around it, the more the target turn competes with its neighbours.
On the short corpus the turn already was almost the whole block, so dilution never
showed up — the window looked like a win. On the full corpus the block carries
long conversations and the turn that matters becomes a minority inside its own
vector.

The part that hurt was understanding why the pilot lied. The small subset had all
items from the same conversation, so no cross-talk was possible: any search hit
because there was only one source. I had measured an improvement on a problem
that did not exist there.

The code that builds the block is short, and it is exactly where dilution is born:

```python
def apply_chunk_window(items, window):

```

One join line. The resulting embedding is the average of the signal of every turn
that made it into the string, and the turn the search should find is just one of
them.

## What changes

The ruler you use to decide has to carry the real difficulty of the problem. An
easy subset produces an optimistic verdict; a subset with broken infrastructure
produces a catastrophic one. Both are false, and the difference between them is
not sample size — it is separating what is statistics from what is environment.
A large negative delta on a change that should be neutral is a symptom of a
broken measurement, not of bad code.

The final decision was to keep the window at zero in production. The multi-hop
bottleneck, the original target of the hypothesis, sits in the same place — which
suggests it is architectural, not a matter of block size. No context window fixes
retrieval that has to connect two distant turns.

## Next step

Before piloting anything new, the measurement corpus needs distinct conversations
by construction — cross-talk mandatory, not accidental. While the pilot runs on
a sample without competition, it will keep approving improvements the full corpus
rejects.
